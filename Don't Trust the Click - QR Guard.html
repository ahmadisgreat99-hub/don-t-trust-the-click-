The problem

A QR code is just a web address drawn as squares. The rules behind QR codes say how to store the address, not who made it. So a criminal's code looks identical to a real one, and your phone opens it after one tap. This trick is called quishing (QR + phishing; phishing means fake messages or sites that trick you into giving away passwords or money).

Why:

    Plain-word guide to the terms
    • Phishing: fake messages or sites that trick you into giving away passwords or money.
    • HTTPS: a locked connection. The lock only means your data is scrambled on the way, not that the site is honest.
    • Look-alike domain: a fake website name that copies a real one, like paypa1.com (number 1 instead of letter l).
    • Short link: links like bit.ly hide where they really go.
    • Raw IP address: a number like 192.168.4.7 instead of a name; real brands do not use these.

    Limits

    QR Guard checks the address text only. It cannot follow redirects or look up how new a website is yet, so a brand-new scam site with a clean-looking address could pass. Its list of trusted brands is a small demo list. Next steps: live redirect checks, domain-age lookup, and a phone app.
